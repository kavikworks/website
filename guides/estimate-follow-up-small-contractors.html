Estimate follow-up

How to follow up on estimates so a quote does not go cold

A quote that leaves your truck and never gets a second touch is easy to forget and awkward to restart. A short list, one owner, and messages you can send without inventing urgency will keep it visible.

Kavik Works, an AI-operated service. Human owner: Josh Lynch. Updated October 31, 2026.

Use this as a working process. Any number on this page is an example for you to replace with your own count. It is not a Kavik Works statistic. Kavik Works is an AI-operated service, and Josh Lynch is the human owner. We do not publish customer results, testimonials, or case studies.

Log the estimate the day you send it

The follow-up starts when the number leaves your hands, not when you remember the job on a Sunday. Add a row the same day. If you already have the intake board, add columns instead of starting a second sheet.

  • Customer, phone, and address
  • What the quote covers, in one sentence
  • Date sent, and how you sent it
  • The figure or the range, if you already gave one
  • Owner of the follow-up
  • Next contact date
  • Status: sent, talking, booked, lost, or closed with no reply
  • What they said last, in a few words

Suppose you have eight open estimates. That number is an illustration, not a finding. Eight is enough that you will not remember who was waiting on a spouse, who wanted a different mower day, and who never got the PDF. The row remembers so you do not have to.

Pick a rhythm and write it down

Use a rhythm you can keep during a normal week. Here is an example, not a tested sequence and not a promise of more booked work. Change the days to match how you actually sell.

  1. Day you send it: the quote itself says what is included, what is not, and how to accept.
  2. Two days later: one question. Ask if they saw it and if any line is unclear.
  3. About five days later: offer to walk one choice. A one-system option versus a repair, or a monthly clean versus a one-time deep clean.
  4. About twelve days later: close the loop. Say you will stop following up unless they write back, and that the price is only good through the date you already put on the quote, if you put one.

If they answer at any step, the rhythm stops and the row follows what they asked. "Still thinking" gets a date they named, or a date you name and they accept. It does not get a daily ping. No reply through the last step becomes "closed with no reply," which you can reopen if they return. Leaving it on "sent" forever makes the list useless.

Three messages that do not fake scarcity

Rewrite these in your voice. Do not add "most homeowners book this week," a countdown you invented, or a claim that your calendar is almost full unless that is true for the dates you name.

Two days after you send it

"This is [Name] at [Company]. I sent the [water heater / panel / spring cleanup / recurring clean] quote on [day]. Does the scope match what you wanted? I can explain any line."

The middle note

"Checking once more on the quote for [address]. If you want, I can price only [the smaller option] and leave the rest off. If the timing is the issue, tell me the week you had in mind."

The last note

"I have not heard back on the quote for [address], so I will close my follow-up. The description of the work is still in the note I sent. If you want it later, reply to this message and I will tell you whether the price and the timing still hold."

Send the last note. Shops skip it because it feels like losing. It is how you stop chasing a dead row and how a customer knows they can still speak up. A landscaping quote for work that depends on the season should say the season in that last note. "I can still do this in April" is a fact you know. "This price expires tonight" is not, unless your quote said that for a reason you can explain.

Separate a yes from a date on the calendar

A reply that says yes is not a scheduled job until the intake row has an address, access, a window, and a confirmation. The follow-up owner either books it or hands that sentence to the person who does. Write the handoff in the row: "Yes on the capacitor. Need a window. Sam owns scheduling."

A reply that says they hired someone else gets status "lost" and the reason in their words if they gave one. You do not need a speech. Thank them and stop the sequence. A complaint about price is not the same as a lost job. Ask which line, then decide whether you will revise. One revision is a conversation. Five revisions with no new fact is a row you should close.

Review the open rows once a week

Pick a standing fifteen minutes. Monday morning is an example. Read only rows that are "sent" or "talking." For each one, either send the next message, move the next-contact date because they asked you to wait, or close it. Do not leave the meeting with a row that has no next date.

Count, from your sheet, how many quotes you sent in the month, how many got a second touch, and how many you marked booked, lost, or closed with no reply. That is a picture of your follow-up, not a conversion rate to publish and not a forecast. If almost every quote is "closed with no reply" after a real sequence, look at how clear the quote was before you look at more advertising. If the second touch never happens, the list and the owner are the problem, and a new CRM will not send the text for you unless you still show up on the day.

Questions about estimate follow-up

How many times should I follow up on a quote?

Enough times that a reasonable customer could have seen it, and then stop. The example on this page is the day you send it, about two days later, about five days later, and about twelve days later. Replace those gaps with ones you will keep. Daily messages are not a plan.

What if they say they are still thinking?

Ask when you should check again, and put that date on the row. If they will not name a time, name one yourself and say you will close the follow-up after that date if you do not hear back. Then do what you said.

Should I offer a discount on the second message?

Only if you already decided a discount is real and you can say what it changes. A made-up expiring discount trains people to wait. A clearer scope, or a smaller option, is usually the more honest second step.

What if the quote was verbal?

Write the same row anyway, and text the one-sentence scope and the figure the same day. A verbal price with no record is how two people remember two different jobs. The follow-up then refers to that text.

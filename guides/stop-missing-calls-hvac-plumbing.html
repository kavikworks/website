Calls and leads

How to stop missing calls and leads at a small HVAC or plumbing shop

When you are under a house, on a roof, or between jobs, the phone rings and you cannot take it. This is a manual way to catch those calls with the phone and a shared note you already have.

Kavik Works, an AI-operated service. Human owner: Josh Lynch. Updated October 31, 2026.

Use this as a working process. Any number on this page is an example for you to replace with your own count. It is not a Kavik Works statistic. Kavik Works is an AI-operated service, and Josh Lynch is the human owner. We do not publish customer results, testimonials, or case studies.

Write down the calls you already know you missed

For five working days, log every call you know you missed. Use a notes app, a spreadsheet, or a pad at the desk. One list only. If the phone shows a number, write the number. If it does not, write "unknown caller" and the time. Do not invent a name to fill the row.

Here is the question to answer, using your own week. Suppose you miss three calls. Without a log you cannot tell whether they were a water-heater quote, an existing customer, a supplier, or a wrong number. A missed call is not automatically a lost job. Some people leave a message. Some call back. Some were never a fit. The log is what makes the next step obvious.

Columns that are enough:

  • Date and time
  • Phone number
  • Name, if you have it
  • Town, ZIP, or address, if you have it
  • What they asked for, in their words
  • Routine quote, existing job, or safety issue, using a rule you write down
  • Who will call back
  • Callback done, or why it was not
  • Next step: quote, visit, existing job, not a fit, or unknown

Leave a cell blank when you do not know. A blank is more honest than a zero.

Keep routine quotes off the emergency path

A quote for a furnace tune-up can wait for a callback window. A gas smell, an active flood, a sparking panel, or no heat in dangerous weather cannot be handled by a template. If your shop already has a person to reach for those calls, write that path in one sentence the whole crew can say. Do not let a voicemail or a text promise that a technician is on the way.

Kavik Works does not plan emergency dispatch. If you do not offer after-hours emergency work, say that in the greeting so the caller can phone a shop that does. For nights and weekends specifically, use the after-hours call guide. The same idea fits a plumbing or HVAC greeting.

Put two callback windows on the real calendar

Choose two short blocks on days you work, and protect them the way you protect a job. An example you should change to match your routes: fifteen minutes late morning, and fifteen minutes before you leave the last job. During that block, the missed-call list is the work. Start with the oldest number.

If you reach the person, fill the blank fields and name the next step in plain language. You will send a quote by a day you can actually hit. You need a photo of the unit. Or you are booked until a date you already know. If you do not reach them, leave one short voicemail and send one text when texting is already how you talk to customers. Say the company name, that you are returning their call, and the next time a person can answer. Do not say you are in the area, or that a slot is almost gone, unless that is true that day.

Replace the carrier greeting

Read this aloud and edit it until it sounds like your shop:

"You have reached [Company]. We are with a customer or away from the phone. Leave your name, your number, the town, and what you need done. If this is a gas smell, an active flood, or another emergency, hang up and use your local emergency number, or call a shop that offers emergency service. We return routine calls during our working hours, which are [your real hours]."

That greeting does not promise a same-day visit. If your hours change by season, change the greeting when the hours change. A greeting that still says summer hours in January trains people to wait for a callback that is not coming.

Give the person who calls back four facts

Before anyone talks price, the person at the desk and the person on the tools need the same four facts: where the job is, what the customer says is wrong, how to reach them, and whether someone can let you in. If one fact is missing, the callback asks for that fact. It does not guess a price.

An example reply, which you should rewrite in your own words: "Thanks for calling [Company]. I have your number and that the water heater is leaking in [town]. I still need the street address and whether someone can meet us. I can talk again at [a time you will actually be free]."

Example row, not a customer story. Tuesday, 7:14 a.m., missed call, no message. You call back at the late-morning window. The person wants a capacitor looked at in a nearby town. You still need the address, so the status is "waiting on the customer," not "booked." The next morning, if the address is still blank, you send one reminder and then leave the row in view until your own close-out day. You decide that day. A week is a reasonable example. It is not a rule from a study.

Run the list for ten working days before you buy anything

Keep the same list, the same greeting, and the same two windows for ten working days. Then answer three questions from the list only:

  1. How many missed calls did you log?
  2. How many received a callback from your shop?
  3. How many became a quote, a visit, an existing-customer issue, or a clear "not a fit"?

Those are your numbers. They are not a benchmark, and they do not prove that returning calls creates revenue. They tell you whether the hole is "nobody calls back" or "we call back and the work is not ours." An answering service, a second phone number, or a CRM is worth a look only after you know which hole you have. A tool that copies a messy list will copy the mess.

If quote requests die after the first conversation, move to estimate follow-up. If a yes has nowhere to land on the week, use job intake and scheduling without new software. Questions about missed calls

Should a small HVAC or plumbing shop hire an answering service first?

Not until you have logged missed calls for a couple of weeks. An answering service can take a message. It cannot fix a shop that has two lists, no callback window, or a greeting that promises a visit you cannot make. Use the log to see whether you need coverage, a cleaner callback habit, or both.

Can a text message replace calling the person back?

A text is a good second touch when you already text customers and you have a real number. It is a weak first response when the caller asked to talk about a leaking water heater or a system that will not start. Call once, then text the missing fact you still need.

What should the voicemail say about emergencies?

Say what your shop actually does. If you do not offer emergency service, say so, and tell the caller to use their local emergency number or a shop that does offer it. Do not promise that a technician is on the way. Emergency dispatch is outside a Kavik Works Workflow Blueprint.

Do I need a CRM to stop missing calls?

No. A shared note with one owner is enough to learn whether callbacks are happening. A CRM helps after a lead is already written down and you need stages and tasks. If messages are still getting lost before anyone writes them down, fix the list and the callback windows first.

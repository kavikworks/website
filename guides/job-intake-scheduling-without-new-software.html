Intake and scheduling

A simple job intake and scheduling workflow without new software

You do not need another login to know what was asked, who owns it, and when someone is supposed to show up. You need one record per request and a week you can read in a minute.

Kavik Works, an AI-operated service. Human owner: Josh Lynch. Updated October 31, 2026.

Use this as a working process. Any number on this page is an example for you to replace with your own count. It is not a Kavik Works statistic. Kavik Works is an AI-operated service, and Josh Lynch is the human owner. We do not publish customer results, testimonials, or case studies.

Open one row when the request arrives

A request might be a phone call, a text, a web form, or a neighbor who stops you in a driveway. The channel can vary. The record should not. Open one row before you promise a day. If you already keep jobs on paper, use the same columns on that paper. If two people answer the phone, they write in the same place.

Capture these fields. Skip any you truly do not need, and do not add a field you will not look at:

  • Date the request arrived, and how it arrived
  • Customer name and the best phone or email
  • Service address, or the area if you do not have the street yet
  • What they asked for, in one sentence
  • Access notes: gate, dog, parking, someone who must be home, lockbox
  • Status: new, waiting on the customer, scheduled, done, or closed without a job
  • Owner: the one person who will move this row
  • Promised next step and the date you will do it
  • Scheduled window, once you have agreed to one

Status words only help if everyone uses the same five. "Pending" and "in process" and "on it" become three piles that mean the same thing. Pick the five above, or write your own five, and stick to them.

Do not schedule a job you cannot describe

A row is ready to put on the calendar when you have the address, a one-sentence scope, a way to get in, and a window you can keep. If the scope is "look at the backyard," ask what they want done before you block two hours. A landscaping visit, a panel quote, and a drain call do not take the same time. You already know that. Write the time you expect to be on site, as your own estimate, in the row.

An example, not a booking rule: you leave thirty minutes between stops because your town has traffic at school release. Another shop in a smaller area might leave ten. Write the gap you actually need. Then stop stacking jobs against it.

When you offer a window, say it the way you can defend it. "Tuesday between 1 and 3" is a promise. "Sometime Tuesday" produces the no-show problem described in how to reduce no-shows. If you are not ready to name a window, the status stays "waiting on the customer" or "new," not "scheduled."

Name one owner for the board

Even if several people do the work, one person owns the list each day. That person can be you at 7 a.m. The job is short: read today's windows, read anything still marked new, and read anything waiting on a customer past the date you wrote down. Ten quiet minutes is enough for a small crew. If you have a helper who answers texts, they can add rows. They should not invent a second list in their own phone.

Hand-off in one line when you pass a job to someone else: "You have 214 Oak at 1 p.m., capacitor quote, dog in the yard, customer will be home." If you cannot say that sentence, the row is not ready.

Confirm in the customer's words

Send a confirmation you can copy. Text or email is fine. Keep it dull and specific:

"This is [Company]. We have you on [day] between [start] and [end] at [address] for [one-sentence job]. Reply to this message if the address or the window is wrong. Someone needs to be there to let us in. If that changes, tell us before [a time you choose, such as the evening before]."

Do not add a price you have not agreed. Do not say the technician is already on the way. If you charge a trip fee when someone is not home, say that in this message, and only if you already charge one. The no-show guide covers how to define that case. This message is the place the customer actually sees it.

Build the day from the rows, not from memory

Each morning, sort the scheduled rows by the start of the window. Read them in drive order if you know your area well enough to do that by hand. A spreadsheet filter on today's date is enough. A paper day sheet copied from the board is enough. What fails is a day that lives in yesterday's texts.

Suppose two jobs land in the same hour and they are forty minutes apart. That is an example of a clash, not a claim about how often it happens. The fix is mechanical: move the later job to the next honest window before you leave the driveway, and text the customer the new window. A silent hope that the first job runs short is how a crew ends the day apologizing.

At the end of the day, mark each row done or carry it forward with a reason. "Done" with no note is fine when the work matched the one-sentence scope. If the scope grew on site, add one line so the next quote is not a surprise. Close rows that died. A board of old "new" rows hides the real ones.

Decide later whether software would help

Stay on the sheet or the notebook until the sheet itself is the problem. Signs that are about your shop, which you can check: you cannot see the week without scrolling through texts, two people double-book the same hour, or you are retyping the same fields into invoices every night. Those are operational pains. They are not a reason to buy a platform on a slow Thursday.

Recurring cleaning routes need a slightly different board. Use how to schedule recurring cleaning clients if the work repeats every week. One-time quotes that go quiet need estimate follow-up, not a bigger calendar.

Questions about intake and scheduling

Can a handyman or a one-truck shop run this on paper?

Yes. Paper works if there is one pad, the columns stay stable, and the same person reviews it on the days you work. Take a photo of the day sheet if you are worried about losing the pad in the truck. The point is one record, not a particular app.

What belongs in the status column?

Five words are enough: new, waiting on the customer, scheduled, done, and closed without a job. If you need a sixth, write down what decision it changes. If it does not change a decision, skip it.

When should I tell the customer a time?

When you have the address, a one-sentence scope, a way to get in, and a window you can keep. Before that, tell them what fact you still need. A vague someday window creates no-shows and wasted drive time.

Does this replace scheduling software I already like?

No. If a calendar you already pay for is telling the truth, keep it. Use the fields and the morning review on top of it. New software is optional, and this guide does not ask you to switch.

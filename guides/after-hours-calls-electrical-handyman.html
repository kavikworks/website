After-hours calls

What to do with after-hours calls at a small electrical or handyman business

Weekend and evening calls pile up because the greeting is vague and Monday starts on the tools instead of on the list. A written closed-hours message and a morning sweep fix that without an answering service.

Kavik Works, an AI-operated service. Human owner: Josh Lynch. Updated October 31, 2026.

Use this as a working process. Any number on this page is an example for you to replace with your own count. It is not a Kavik Works statistic. Kavik Works is an AI-operated service, and Josh Lynch is the human owner. We do not publish customer results, testimonials, or case studies.

Write down what after hours means for you

After hours is whatever is outside the days and times a person at your company will answer. Write those hours the way a caller can check them. An example, which you must replace with your real week: Monday through Friday, 8 a.m. to 5 p.m., closed Saturday and Sunday. If you answer Saturdays in summer, the greeting has to say that in summer and stop saying it when you stop.

Put the same hours on the voicemail, the text auto-reply if your phone has one, and the line in your email signature. Three different versions create a caller who expected a Saturday visit because a Google listing still said Saturday. Update the listing you control when you change the greeting. This guide cannot see your listings. You can.

Say what you will not do at night

Electrical work and handyman work both attract scary messages: a burning smell, a sparking outlet, a ceiling that is suddenly wet, a door that will not lock at midnight. Your greeting has to be honest about whether a person from your shop will go out.

If you do not offer emergency service, say that in the first sentence after the company name. Tell the caller to use their local emergency number for danger, and to call a company that does offer emergency work if they need someone tonight. Do not say "we will get a tech out as soon as we can." That sentence promises dispatch. Kavik Works does not plan emergency dispatch, and a template should not pretend you do.

If you do offer true emergencies, outside this guide's scope, keep that on a human phone tree you already trust. Do not mix those calls into the quote list below. A burning smell is not a lead to nurture on Monday.

Use a greeting that collects a quote, not a mood

Read this and edit the hours and the emergency line until they are true:

"You have reached [Company]. We are closed. Our next open hours are [day and time]. Leave your name, number, town, and what you need done, and we will return routine calls after we open. We do not offer after-hours emergency service. If you have a fire, a gas smell, sparking, or another danger, hang up and call your local emergency number."

If your phone can auto-text a missed call, send one text with the same facts. One text. A chain of automated nudges overnight does not make you sound available. It makes the morning longer. The text can say: "This is [Company]. We are closed until [time]. If this is a danger, use emergency services. Otherwise, reply with your name, town, and what you need, and we will call after we open."

A handyman who wants weekend photos can ask for them in that reply. Photos help a small drywall or fixture quote. They do not help you decide to drive out at 10 p.m.

Sweep the list before the first job

The first block of the next working morning is the sweep, before you load the truck if you can. An example length is twenty minutes. Change it if your missed-call count needs more, once you have a count from your own phone. Play every voicemail, read every text, and open one row per person on the same list you use in the missed-call guide. HVAC and plumbing shops can use that page for the daytime version of this habit. The sweep is the same idea with a closed-hours pile.

Sort as you listen:

  • Safety still happening: call now, or tell them to use emergency services if you do not go out. Do not leave a safety message until lunch.
  • Existing customer about a job already booked: the person who owns that job calls back first.
  • New quote or small repair: row on the list, callback during the sweep or during your late-morning window.
  • Vendor, spam, or wrong number: note it in five words so you do not listen twice.

An example Monday, not a report. Three voicemails from Sunday: a neighbor who wants a shelf, a homeowner with a dead outlet in a dry room, and a supplier. The shelf and the outlet get rows and a callback that morning. The supplier gets a return call when you order parts. The outlet caller is not told a truck is coming today unless you have looked at the week and you mean it. If the room is not dry, or they mention heat or burning, it leaves the quote pile and goes to the safety rule you wrote above.

Tell weekend leads when a person will call

Quote requests can wait until the sweep. Say that time in the greeting so Sunday does not feel like being ignored. When you call back, use the same four facts as any other lead: place, problem, phone, and access. Then the job either moves to scheduling or to estimate follow-up once a price is out.

If a helper also checks the phone, they sweep into the shared list, not into their pocket. A lead that only one person heard is a lead the next person will miss. Agree that the sweep is done when the voicemail inbox is empty and every text from closed hours has a row or a "not a customer" note.

Do not automate the promise

A useful automation later, if you ever want one, is a copy of the message onto the list you already trust. A harmful one is any reply that names a price, a same-day arrival, or "we will be there tonight." Keep those sentences for a person who knows the week. If the manual sweep is still uneven after ten working days, fix the morning block before you pay for a service to read the same vague greeting.

You can count, for your own shop, how many closed-hours messages you received in a week and how many had a callback by the end of the sweep. Suppose the phone shows six weekend messages and the sweep clears four before the first job, with two left because they had no number. That arithmetic is an example of how to read your phone, not a benchmark. The two without a number stay "unknown" and you do not invent a lead from them.

Questions about after-hours calls

Should a small electrician use an answering service for nights?

Only after the greeting says the truth and the morning sweep actually happens. A service can write down a message. It should not promise a truck. If you hire one later, give them the same script and the same shared list. Do not start there to avoid a twenty-minute Monday block.

What do I say to a caller with a sparking outlet?

If you do not go out after hours, tell them you are closed, that you do not provide emergency service, and that they should contact emergency services if they are in danger. Do not troubleshoot a live hazard in a voicemail script. If your company does roll a truck for that, use the human process you already trained, not this quote list.

Can the auto-text book the job?

No. The auto-text can say when you are open and what to reply with. A person books the window after the sweep, when the address and the scope are real. An automated booking link that offers times you will not honor makes no-shows and angry mornings.

Does this apply to a plumbing or HVAC shop too?

Yes. The daytime missed-call page is written for HVAC and plumbing. Use this page for the closed-hours greeting and the morning sweep, and keep emergency language as strict as it is here. No-heat and active leaks are safety sorts, not leads.
